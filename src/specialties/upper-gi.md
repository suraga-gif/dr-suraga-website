## What are upper GI cancers?

The upper gastrointestinal (GI) tract carries food from your mouth to your small intestine. The two main cancers in this area are:

- **Oesophageal cancer**, in the food pipe that runs from the throat to the stomach
- **Stomach cancer**, also called gastric cancer

## Warning signs

See a doctor if any of these last for more than two to three weeks:

- Difficulty swallowing, or a feeling that food is getting stuck
- Losing weight without trying
- Indigestion, burning or upper abdominal pain that does not settle with the usual medicines
- Feeling full after eating very little
- Vomiting, especially vomiting blood, or black, tar-like stools
- Tiredness caused by low haemoglobin (anaemia)

Most of the time these symptoms have a less serious cause, but they always need to be checked.

## How it is diagnosed

- **Endoscopy.** A thin, flexible camera is passed through the mouth to look inside the food pipe and stomach. A small piece of tissue (a biopsy) is taken from anything abnormal.
- **CT scan** of the chest and abdomen, and sometimes a **PET-CT scan**, to check whether the cancer has spread.
- **Endoscopic ultrasound** for some patients, to measure how deep the tumour goes.
- **Staging laparoscopy** for some stomach cancers: a short keyhole procedure to check the lining of the abdomen before a major operation.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the stage of the cancer and on your general health. Surgeons, medical oncologists and radiation oncologists plan it together.

- **Surgery** removes the tumour together with the nearby lymph nodes. For the food pipe this operation is called an oesophagectomy. For the stomach it is a partial or total gastrectomy.
- **Chemotherapy, with or without radiotherapy,** is often given before surgery to shrink the cancer, and sometimes afterwards.
- **Very early cancers** can sometimes be removed through an endoscope, without an operation.

### Keyhole and robotic surgery

Many oesophagus and stomach operations can be done through small cuts using laparoscopic (keyhole) or robotic surgery. For suitable patients this can mean less pain and a quicker recovery than open surgery. Dr. Suraga will explain whether it is right for you.

## Recovery and life after surgery

- You start with liquids and move slowly to small, frequent meals.
- A dietitian helps you adjust what and how you eat.
- Regular follow-up visits and scans check that the cancer has not come back.
