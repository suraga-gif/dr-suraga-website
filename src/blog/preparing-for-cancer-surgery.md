Once a date is fixed for cancer surgery, many families feel there's nothing to do but wait and worry. Relatives arrive, everyone has advice, and the patient is often told to rest as much as possible.

In fact, the weeks before an operation are valuable. What you do in this time can make a real difference to how quickly and safely you recover. This guide to **preparing for cancer surgery** covers the steps that help most, along with the practical planning that's easy to forget.

> Think of the weeks before surgery as training, not waiting.

## Why preparing for cancer surgery matters

A cancer operation puts a lot of strain on the body. People who go into surgery a little fitter, better nourished and free of tobacco generally find it easier to get out of bed, breathe deeply and eat again afterwards. Getting your body ready in this way is sometimes called **prehabilitation**.

Many hospitals now follow **enhanced recovery** programmes, sometimes called ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery). These are care plans designed to get you moving, eating and home sooner. Your own preparation is the first part of that plan.

## Stop tobacco now, in every form

**Smoking, beedi, gutka, khaini and paan** all slow down healing. Smoking also makes chest infections after surgery more likely. Stopping even a few weeks before your operation helps, so don't think it's "too late to matter".

If you find it hard to stop, tell your doctor. Support and medicines to help with quitting are available.

**Alcohol** can also interfere with healing and with anaesthesia. Tell your team honestly how much you drink, so they can plan for it safely. Don't stop suddenly if you drink heavily every day without speaking to a doctor first, as this can cause withdrawal symptoms.

## Get moving every day

**Walking** is the simplest exercise and one of the most useful. Aim to walk a little every day, and slowly increase the distance. If you already walk regularly, keep going.

**Breathing exercises** help your lungs after surgery. Practise slow, deep breaths a few times a day. Your team may give you a small breathing device called an **incentive spirometer** and show you how to use it.

If you have heart or lung problems, or get breathless easily, ask your doctor how much activity is safe for you.

## Eat well, especially protein

Your body needs extra energy and **protein** to heal wounds and keep muscles strong. Good sources include dal, eggs, milk and curd, paneer, fish, chicken and pulses.

**This is not the time to diet.** Some families try to "lighten" the patient before surgery by cutting down food. If you've been losing weight or eating less because of the illness, tell your doctor. A dietitian can suggest ways to eat more, or supplements if needed.

**If you have diabetes,** good sugar control before surgery lowers the chance of wound infection. Keep taking your medicines as prescribed and check your sugars regularly.

## Medicines: tell your team about everything

At the pre-surgery check, bring a full list of everything you take, including:

- Tablets and injections for diabetes, blood pressure or heart problems
- **Blood thinners** (medicines that prevent clots)
- Ayurvedic, homeopathic or herbal medicines, and supplements

Some of these may need to be adjusted before surgery. **Never stop any medicine on your own.** Your surgeon or anaesthetist will tell you exactly what to stop, what to continue and when.

## The pre-surgery check

Before your operation, you'll usually have a **pre-anaesthetic check-up**. The anaesthetist (the doctor who puts you to sleep for surgery) reviews your health, orders blood tests, an ECG and sometimes a chest X-ray or heart scan, and explains the anaesthesia plan.

This is a good time to ask questions. You'll also get clear instructions about when to stop eating and drinking before surgery. Follow these exactly, because they're there for your safety.

## Plan the practical side early

- **Who will stay with you?** Decide which family member will be with you in hospital and at home for the first week or two.
- **Insurance and documents:** if you're using health insurance or a government scheme, start the pre-authorisation process early and keep copies of your reports, ID and policy papers together. Check the details with the hospital's insurance desk.
- **Time off work:** ask your surgeon roughly how long you'll need, and plan with your employer.
- **What to pack:** loose comfortable clothes, slippers with a good grip, your medicine list, reports and a phone charger. Leave jewellery and valuables at home.
- **Travelling from another town?** Ask whether you'll need to stay nearby for follow-up visits after discharge.

## When to contact your team before surgery

**Call your surgeon's team** if, before your operation, you develop a fever, cough or cold, a new infection, chest pain, or if your symptoms suddenly get worse. Your surgery may need to be adjusted for your safety.

**Go to an emergency department straight away** if you have severe breathlessness, chest pain, vomiting blood or heavy bleeding.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I rest completely before my operation?

No. Gentle, regular activity like walking helps your body cope with surgery and recover afterwards. Rest when you're tired, but try not to spend the days lying in bed.

### Is it too late to stop smoking if my surgery is next week?

It's never too late. Stopping even a short time before surgery helps, and staying off tobacco after surgery helps your wound and lungs heal. Ask your team for help to quit.

### Can I eat normally until the day of surgery?

Usually yes, and eating well is encouraged. The hospital will tell you when to stop eating and drinking before the operation. Follow their timing exactly.

### What questions should I ask my surgeon?

Useful questions include: what the operation involves, whether it will be open, keyhole or robotic surgery, how long you'll be in hospital, what recovery at home looks like, and when you can return to work. Write them down and bring them to your appointment.
