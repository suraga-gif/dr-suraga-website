You went for an ultrasound of the neck, or a scan for something else entirely, and the report mentions a "thyroid nodule". Or you noticed a small swelling in the front of your neck while looking in the mirror. Either way, the first question in your mind is usually the same: is this cancer?

So, is a thyroid nodule cancer? In most cases, the answer is no. Thyroid nodules are very common, and the large majority are harmless. This article explains how doctors tell the two apart, what the tests involve, and when a **thyroid nodule** needs more than a watchful eye.

> Most thyroid nodules are found by chance, and most of them simply need watching.

## What is a thyroid nodule?

The **thyroid** is a butterfly-shaped gland at the front of the neck, just below the Adam's apple. It makes hormones that control how fast your body uses energy.

A **nodule** is a lump that forms within the thyroid. It can be solid, filled with fluid (a cyst), or a mix of both. Some people have one nodule, others have several. When the whole gland is enlarged, it's often called a **goitre**.

Nodules become more common with age and are seen more often in women. Many are only found because modern ultrasound machines can spot very small lumps that would never have caused any problem.

## Is a thyroid nodule cancer? Why most are not

Most nodules are made of normal thyroid tissue that has grown a little unevenly, or are simple fluid-filled cysts. These don't spread and usually don't need surgery.

A small proportion of nodules do turn out to be thyroid cancer. Even then, the most common types of thyroid cancer usually grow slowly and are among the most treatable cancers, especially when found early.

## Signs that make doctors look more closely

Your doctor will ask questions and examine your neck. These features raise the level of concern and usually mean tests should be done sooner rather than later:

- The lump is **growing quickly** over weeks or a few months
- It feels **hard or fixed**, rather than soft and moving when you swallow
- Your **voice has become hoarse** and stays that way
- You have **difficulty swallowing or breathing**
- There are **swollen glands (lymph nodes)** on the side of the neck
- You had **radiation to the head or neck** in childhood
- A close relative has had **thyroid cancer**

Having one of these doesn't mean you have cancer. It means the nodule deserves a careful check.

## The tests: what each one tells you

**Thyroid blood test (TSH).** This checks whether your thyroid is making too much or too little hormone. Nodules that make too much hormone are rarely cancerous, so this result helps decide the next step.

**Ultrasound of the neck.** This is the most useful test. It shows the size of the nodule and what it looks like inside. Features such as very irregular edges, tiny bright specks (microcalcifications) or a nodule that is taller than it is wide raise suspicion. Many radiologists now give a score, often called a **TI-RADS** score, that summarises how concerning the nodule looks. <!-- REVIEW: confirm TI-RADS is the scoring system commonly reported by radiologists in Bengaluru (some use ATA patterns) -->

**FNAC (fine needle aspiration cytology).** If the ultrasound looks suspicious, or the nodule is above a certain size, a very thin needle is used to draw out a few cells for testing. It's done in the clinic, often with ultrasound guidance, and takes a few minutes. Most people feel only a pinch, similar to a blood test. No stitches are needed, and you can go home straight away.

**Understanding the FNAC report.** Results are usually reported in one of six categories (the Bethesda system), ranging from clearly harmless to clearly cancer. Some results fall in the middle and are called "indeterminate". These may need a repeat FNAC, further testing, or sometimes surgery to remove the nodule and look at it fully. <!-- REVIEW: check the brief description of Bethesda categories -->

## A common misunderstanding: "The needle test will spread the cancer"

This fear stops many people from having an FNAC. A fine needle test is a standard, safe procedure, and it doesn't cause thyroid cancer to spread. Skipping it can delay a clear answer, which is the bigger risk.

## What happens next?

**If the nodule looks harmless,** your doctor will usually suggest a repeat ultrasound after some months to make sure it's stable. Many people never need anything more.

**If the nodule is large or causing pressure,** even when it's harmless, surgery may be offered to relieve swallowing or breathing difficulty, or for cosmetic reasons.

**If FNAC shows cancer or strongly suspects it,** surgery is usually recommended. This may mean removing half of the thyroid (hemithyroidectomy) or all of it (total thyroidectomy), depending on the size and type of the cancer. Some patients need radioactive iodine treatment afterwards.

For suitable patients, thyroid surgery can now be done robotically, without a cut on the front of the neck. The [Head and neck cancers](/specialties/head-and-neck/) page explains this in more detail.

## When to see a doctor

**Book an appointment soon** if you notice a new lump in the front of your neck, or a scan report mentions a thyroid nodule that hasn't been checked.

**See a doctor within days** if you have a neck lump along with a hoarse voice that hasn't improved in two to three weeks, difficulty swallowing, or swollen glands in the neck.

**Go to an emergency department straight away** if a neck swelling makes it hard to breathe.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a thyroid nodule go away on its own?

Fluid-filled nodules sometimes shrink or disappear. Solid nodules usually stay, but most remain the same size for years. That's why repeat ultrasounds are often all that's needed.

### Does a thyroid nodule affect my thyroid hormone levels?

Usually not. Most nodules don't change hormone levels. A small number make extra hormone, which the TSH blood test will show.

### Will I need to take thyroid tablets after surgery?

If the whole thyroid is removed, you'll need to take a thyroid hormone tablet every day for life. After half the gland is removed, many people don't need tablets, but your blood levels will be checked to be sure.

### Is it safe to wait and watch?

For nodules that look harmless on ultrasound and FNAC, careful follow-up is a safe and widely used approach. Your doctor will tell you how often to repeat the scan.
